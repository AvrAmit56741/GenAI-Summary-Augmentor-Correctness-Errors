# Generator freshness in evaluation-metric papers: Machine Translation and Code

Compiled 2026-11-18. Scope: papers that propose a new MT or code-generation evaluation metric or benchmark, plus the WMT Metrics shared-task findings (2022–2025). For each paper the question is: **which systems produced the outputs the metric was validated on**, and how fresh, large, open and frontier those systems were compared with the paper's own date.

**How each fact was checked.** Unless a cell is marked otherwise, the fact comes from the paper's full text. I downloaded the PDF from the ACL Anthology or arXiv and extracted the text. The markers are:
- **(mem)**: from memory. Plausible, but not checked against the paper in this pass.
- **(unverified)**: the paper does not say, or the source could not be confirmed.
- Model release dates and sizes come from general knowledge **(mem)** unless the paper itself gives them. Two papers do: Wang et al. 2025 (Table 1) and the WMT25 ranking (sizes).

**Definitions.**
- **Generators**: the systems whose outputs were scored and correlated with human or execution-based gold labels.
- **Judge / metric LLM**: an LLM used *as* the metric. These are listed separately.
- **Age gap** = the paper's year (first public version) minus the release year of the newest generator.
- **Frontier?** = whether the strongest generally available system for the task *at the paper's date* was among the generators.

### 1.4 MT patterns

- **Typical age gap.** Papers that propose an MT metric outside the shared task almost always validate on *last year's* WMT MQM release, so the gap is **≈1 year**. Examples: BLEURT and COMET on WMT19; GEMBA, AutoMQM and xCOMET on WMT22. The gap goes to **0** only through the shared task itself, where metrics are run blind on the current year's submissions. Shared-task submissions (COMET-22, MetricX-23/24, GEMBA-MQM) report both: in-paper experiments on year T−1 and official results on year T.
- **Yearly test-set refresh.** WMT renews its test sets every year with new sources, new systems and new MQM annotations. Language pairs rotate: en-ru was replaced by **he-en** in 2023, and ja-zh and en-es were added in 2024. Since 2023 the organisers deliberately *add* frontier LLMs that nobody submitted: GPT4-5shot in 2023; GPT-4, Claude-3.5, Gemini-1.5-Pro, Llama3-70B, Aya23, Mistral-Large and CommandR+ in 2024; and about 20 LLMs in 2025. This yearly refresh is the field's main defence against generator-staleness. **No comparable mechanism exists for summarization factual-consistency benchmarks.**
- **Frontier and closed inclusion.** From 2023 on, closed frontier LLMs are always included through WMT, alongside open models from 7B to 671B. Before 2023 the "frontier" consisted of closed commercial online engines (Online-A/B/W…) plus top research NMT.
- **Sizes.** Pre-2023 generators were NMT models of roughly 0.1–1B parameters (mem). From 2023 they range from 7B open models to undisclosed-size closed models. WMT22 deliberately included *weaker* systems (Transformer-big MBR) to elicit errors, and WMT24/25 add low-quality systems (MSLC) to widen the quality range.
- **Evidence on metric quality versus generator strength.**
  - (a) Long-standing evidence that metrics struggle to rank *top* systems: Mathur 2020 (mem); COMET §5.4; BLEURT's "quality drift" section; Agrawal 2024 (high-quality translations).
  - (b) WMT24 explicitly asked "Are LLMs breaking MT metrics?" and answered **no** for fine-tuned neural metrics.
  - (c) WMT23 and WMT24 show that human references can be *worse* than frontier LLM output, which breaks reference-based metrics. WMT23's subtitle is "References Are Not Innocent."
  - (d) LLM judges can favour their own family (GEMBA-MQM with GPT-4 on GPT-4 translations; Agrawal 2024).
  - (e) WMT25 finds LLM judges best at the system level and worst at the segment level. Its data was chosen to be hard for LLM translators.

### 2.2 Code patterns

- **Typical age gap.** There are two eras.
  - **2020–2024 reference-based and early-LLM-judge metrics** (CodeBLEU, CodeBERTScore, ICE-Score, CodeJudge) were validated on the **same small, aging human-rated sets**: CoNaLa (Evtikhiev's 5 models from 2018–2021) and HumanEval outputs from code-davinci-002 (2022). This sets a gap of **1–3 years** at publication. The gap widens with every reuse: CodeJudge (2024) still validates on 2018–2021 CoNaLa outputs. ICE-Score's judges (GPT-3.5/GPT-4) were *stronger and newer* than every generator they judged.
  - **2024–2025 judge benchmarks** (CodeUltraFeedback, CodeJudge-Eval, CodeJudgeBench) generate fresh outputs from current models, giving a gap of **≈0–0.5 years**.
- **No institutional yearly refresh** like WMT. Freshness depends on each paper's authors. LiveCodeBench (time-stamped problems) is used to avoid contamination of the *problems*, not to keep the *generators* current.
- **Frontier and closed inclusion.** This is mixed. It is absent in CodeBERTScore and ICE-Score (Codex only), in Wang et al. ISSTA 2025 (open ≤34B only), and unverifiable in CodeJudge. It is present in CodeUltraFeedback (GPT-4-Turbo), CodeJudge-Eval (GPT-4, Claude-3-Opus) and CodeJudgeBench (Gemini-2.5-Pro, Claude-4-Opus).
- **Sizes.** Pre-2023 generators were ≤1.5B open models or Codex. In 2024–25 they range from 1.3B to 70B open models plus closed frontier models.
- **Ground truth.** Execution (unit tests) is the dominant gold label in code (HumanEval, APPS, BigCodeBench, LiveCodeBench). It is objective, which makes the generator-dependence findings especially telling: CodeJudgeBench argues that "code correctness is an objective criterion," yet judge accuracy varies by generator.
- **Evidence of metric degradation on stronger generators.** CodeJudgeBench is the clearest case. Judges that are near-perfect on benchmarks built from GPT-4o-era outputs drop substantially on outputs from Gemini-2.5 and Claude-3.7/4, because frontier errors are "subtle." Judge accuracy also varies across generators: e.g. "QwQ is much better at judging responses from Claude-3.7-Sonnet than Gemini-2.5-Pro/Flash." CRUXEval shows that HumanEval-style scores of GPT-4-distilled models overstate their ability.

## 3. Cross-task takeaways for our paper (Hebrew summarization factual consistency)

1. **MT is the clearest positive example of the practice.** WMT refreshes test sets yearly and, since 2023, deliberately adds the current closed frontier LLMs (GPT-4, Claude-3.5, Gemini-1.5/2.5, GPT-4.1, Claude-4) and open LLMs (7B–671B) as translation systems. Metric papers then report on outputs with an age gap of 0–1 years. We can cite WMT22–25 as the norm we follow by scoring outputs from current models.
2. **WMT23 included Hebrew→English with GPT-4 outputs**, and GPT4-5shot was the best he→en system (MQM 1.33 against 1.17 for the human reference). That is a Hebrew-related precedent for including frontier LLM outputs in a metric meta-evaluation.
3. **Evidence that metric quality depends on generator strength:**
   - MT: degradation when ranking top systems (BLEURT quality drift; COMET top-N; Agrawal 2024); references worse than LLM output (WMT23/24); a judge's self-preference for its own family's outputs (GEMBA-MQM with GPT-4).
   - Counter-evidence: WMT24 reports *no* breakdown of fine-tuned neural metrics on LLM translations.
   - Code: CodeJudgeBench shows judge accuracy collapsing on outputs from frontier generators, and variance across generators.
4. **Common weak practice to contrast with:** reusing old human-rated sets for years. In code, CoNaLa (2018–2021 generators) was still used for validation in 2024 (CodeJudge). In non-shared-task MT papers, last year's WMT MQM is reused (xCOMET, GEMBA, AutoMQM on WMT22).
5. **Judge versus generator separation.** Several papers use a judge that is stronger or newer than the generators (ICE-Score: GPT-4 judging Codex output; GEMBA: GPT-4 judging WMT22 NMT). The reverse also occurs (CodeUltraFeedback: GPT-3.5 judging GPT-4-Turbo). WMT25 and CodeJudgeBench are the cases where judge and generator families coincide.
